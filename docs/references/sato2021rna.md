### RNA Secondary Structure Prediction with Deep Learning and Thermodynamic Integration (MXfold2)

Accurately predicting RNA secondary structure is crucial because many non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) perform their functions through structure rather than primary sequence alone. Experimental structure determination (NMR, X-ray, even cryo-EM) is still expensive and limited in throughput, so computational prediction of base-pairing patterns (hairpins, internal loops, bulges, multiloops, etc.) is widely used.

The classical approach relies on thermodynamic nearest-neighbor models such as Turner’s parameters, in which an RNA secondary structure is decomposed into standard loop types (hairpin, internal, bulge, stacking, multibranch, external; illustrated in Fig. 1 on p. 2) and the total free energy is obtained by summing loop contributions. An optimal (minimum free-energy) structure can then be computed via Zuker-style dynamic programming, as implemented in tools like Mfold/UNAFold, ViennaRNA (RNAfold), and RNAstructure.

Machine-learning–based methods replace experimentally measured energy parameters with learned scores for substructures. Systems such as CONTRAfold, ContextFold, and TORNADO train rich parameter sets on sequence–structure pairs and can surpass purely thermodynamic models in accuracy, but they are prone to overfitting, especially when training and test families differ. Hybrid methods like SimFold and MXfold attempt to combine thermodynamic and learned parameters, using experimental energies as a physically grounded backbone while letting data-driven weights refine frequently observed motifs.

More recently, deep neural networks have been applied directly to RNA secondary structure prediction. SPOT-RNA and E2Efold treat prediction as a matrix of binary classification problems—one for each nucleotide pair (i, j)—and use large DNNs to decide whether (i, j) forms a base pair. These models can achieve high accuracy on sequences similar to their training data, but their heavy parameterization makes them especially vulnerable to overfitting and poor generalization to new RNA families.

MXfold2 proposes a deep learning approach that explicitly integrates thermodynamics to improve robustness. The model’s neural network takes an RNA sequence, embeds each nucleotide, runs several 1D convolutional blocks (Block 1), followed by BiLSTM layers (Block 2), and then lifts the sequence representation to a 2D L×L feature map via a “Concat 2D” operation. This is processed by 2D convolutional blocks (Block 3), and finally a 3-layer MLP outputs four folding scores for each nucleotide pair (i, j): helix stacking, helix opening, helix closing, and unpaired region (network overview in Fig. 2 on p. 3). These scores are then used to score nearest-neighbor loops, while Turner free energies are added as an additional term, so the total score of a structure is the sum of neural and thermodynamic contributions.

Decoding still uses Zuker-style dynamic programming, ensuring that MXfold2 inherits the efficient exact search over pseudoknot-free nearest-neighbor structures. The key algorithmic novelty lies in the training objective: parameters are optimized with a structured SVM (max-margin) loss over all possible secondary structures, augmented with a *thermodynamic regularization* term that penalizes discrepancies between the network’s folding score and the thermodynamic free energy of the same structure, plus standard ℓ² weight decay. This encourages the learned scores to remain consistent with known physical energetics, reducing the tendency to memorize training families.

Ablation experiments compare a “base” DNN-only model to variants that add thermodynamic integration and/or thermodynamic regularization. On the Rivas *TrainSetA/TestSetA/TestSetB* benchmark, the pure DNN achieves high F-score on structurally similar TestSetA but performs poorly on structurally dissimilar TestSetB, indicating heavy overfitting. Adding both thermodynamic integration and regularization yields the final MXfold2 model, which maintains strong accuracy on TestSetA and substantially improves performance on TestSetB, demonstrating more robust generalization to unseen families.

When compared to a broad set of existing tools—including MXfold, TORNADO, CONTRAfold, ContextFold, RNAfold, SimFold, and LinearFold—MXfold2 attains the highest F-scores on both structurally similar and dissimilar test sets, and on the combined benchmark. ContextFold, for example, performs well on familiar structures but degrades sharply on TestSetB, whereas MXfold2 remains stable.

The authors also benchmark MXfold2 against deep learning baselines SPOT-RNA and E2Efold using the bpRNA-1m–derived TR0/TS0 split (sequence-wise cross-validation) and a new bpRNA-new dataset containing only families absent from training (family-wise cross-validation). Sequence-wise, MXfold2 is comparable to SPOT-RNA, but in family-wise evaluation MXfold2 significantly outperforms SPOT-RNA and shows far better robustness than E2Efold, which nearly fails on unseen families. MXfold2 is also substantially faster than SPOT-RNA (≈15× on GPU, ≈36× on CPU on average).

Finally, using the T-Full dataset of sequence–structure–energy triplets, the authors show that MXfold2’s folding scores correlate well with experimentally measured free energies, performing markedly better than CONTRAfold and approaching RNAfold’s correlation. Removing thermodynamic regularization leaves secondary-structure accuracy almost unchanged but dramatically worsens this energy correlation, underscoring that the regularization term is what ties the DNN scores back to physically meaningful energy values.

Overall, MXfold2 illustrates an important design pattern for RNA structure prediction: deep neural networks can provide flexible, high-capacity scoring, but integrating them with thermodynamic priors and regularization is crucial for generalization to novel RNA families and for retaining physically interpretable energy estimates. This is conceptually relevant to motif-level or 3D tasks as well—suggesting that for limited, heterogeneous RNA datasets (like specific motif classes or cryo-EM fragments), combining data-driven models with explicit biophysical constraints may yield more robust and biologically meaningful predictions than deep learning alone.
